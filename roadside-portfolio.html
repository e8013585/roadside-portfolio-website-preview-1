Field notes — 2026

What we're building on right now

01

Texture over polish

Grain, stamped edges, and hand-set kerning are back — identities that admit they were made by someone, not generated. We build that imperfection in on purpose.

02

Distinction, not novelty

Marks are judged on whether they mean something specific to the business, not how unusual they look. Gothic stamps, crests, and foundry marks are getting a second look for exactly this reason.

03

Designed for a thumbnail

Half of a quick-service brand's first impression now happens at 32 pixels, cropped into a circle, on a delivery app. That constraint shapes the mark before anything else does.

04

Warm, saturated, appetite-first

Restaurant and food palettes are trading pastel neutrals for real color — rust, cherry, marigold — because warmth reads as flavor from across a parking lot.

Job No. 001 — Ember & Grove Category — Full-Service Restaurant Scope — Identity, Signage, Print, Environmental

Casefile 001

ember & grove

A single wood-fired hearth, family-style boards, and the oak grove out back that feeds it. West Asheville, North Carolina.

Location1204 Haywood Road, Asheville, NC
Founded2024
Chef / OwnerMarisol Doyle
ScopeIdentity, menu system, signage, wraps, merch

Marisol Doyle spent six years cooking over coals in San Sebastián before coming home to the Blue Ridge. Ember & Grove is built around one piece of equipment: a wood-fired hearth burning oak and hickory cut from a five-acre grove the restaurant leases twenty minutes up the road. Everything on the menu passes over that fire.

The brief asked for a mark that could hold both halves of that story — the fire and the wood — without turning into a campfire cliché. We landed on an ember nested inside an oak leaf: the leaf is the grove, the glow inside it is the hearth. The ampersand between the two words carries a small upward flick, the only place in the wordmark where the fire shows up in the type itself.

The palette runs darker and warmer than the usual restaurant cream-and-terracotta pairing — char black is the dominant ground, not an accent, because the dining room itself is dim, lit mostly by the hearth. Bone and ember only show up where the fire would actually reach.

Job No. 002 — Pronto Bird Category — Fast-Casual, Quick-Service Scope — Identity, Packaging, Delivery, Signage

Casefile 002

pronto bird

Nashville-hot-adjacent fried chicken sandwiches, built app-first. East Austin, Texas.

Location2114 E 6th St, Austin, TX
Founded2022, as a food truck
FoundersDeja Marsh & Tobias Kim
ScopeIdentity, packaging, app icon, signage, wraps

Deja Marsh and Tobias Kim ran a chicken sandwich truck out of the Meanwhile Truck Yard for two years before opening a brick-and-mortar with a drive-thru window built for delivery drivers, not dine-in traffic. Ninety percent of Pronto Bird's orders now happen through a phone screen — which meant the logo had a harder job than most: survive being cropped into a circle at 32 pixels next to nine other restaurant icons.

The rooster head mark was drawn to that constraint first. The comb is three flat angled tabs instead of a curved crest — curves disappear at thumbnail size, angles don't. The beak is a single triangle. Nothing in the mark depends on a line thinner than it needs to be to survive a phone screen.

Spice levels are named for weather — Breeze, Gust, Storm, Squall — which gave the packaging system a second layer of color-coding beyond the core palette, without touching the logo itself.

Job No. 003 — Ironclad Auto & Cycle Category — Repair & Restoration Scope — Identity, Signage, Print, Fleet

Casefile 003

Ironclad

Third-generation repair shop, now doing EV diagnostics alongside classic motorcycle restoration. Corktown, Detroit.

Location1889 Bagley Street, Detroit, MI
Founded1962, by Wesley Okafor Sr.
Current ownerWesley Okafor III
ScopeIdentity, work orders, signage, fleet, merch

Wesley Okafor Sr. opened the shop in 1962, two blocks from the old Michigan Central Station, fixing whatever the neighborhood's plant workers drove home. Sixty-plus years and two generations later, his grandson runs the same bay — now split between EV battery diagnostics and ground-up motorcycle restorations, sometimes in the same afternoon.

The family didn't want a mark that chased a trend. They wanted something that looked like it belonged on the building in 1962 and still would in 2062 — so we went to the source: old Detroit machine-shop foundry stamps, the kind pressed directly into cast parts. The crest is a pointed-arch shield, the shape those stamps actually used, built around a wrench driven through a hex nut.

Everything after that follows the same logic — navy and safety orange, because that's what was already on the shop's toolboxes and the OSHA signage bolted to the wall, not because it tested well in a mood board.
